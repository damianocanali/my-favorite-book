## consent-age

Headline: "under 13" is WRONG for Italy. Italy set the GDPR Art. 8 age of digital consent at 14 (art. 2-quinquies, D.Lgs. 196/2003, inserted by D.Lgs. 101/2018), not 13. "Under 13" is the COPPA (US) number and has no basis in Italian law.

But the more important finding is that fixing "13" to "14" is probably still the wrong fix. GDPR Art. 8 engages only under two CUMULATIVE conditions, both in the text of Art. 8(1): (i) the lawful basis is consent under Art. 6(1)(a), AND (ii) the information society service is offered DIRECTLY TO A CHILD. In My Book Lab the account is created and controlled by a parent/guardian/teacher, and most of the processing (making the book, printing and shipping it, running the subscription) is contract-necessary under Art. 6(1)(b). On that reading Art. 8 does not engage for the core service at all, and the policy should stop framing compliance around an "age below which parental consent is needed". The Irish DPC states this plainly: "if they are using a different legal basis, the age of digital consent does not apply."

What does NOT go away is that the app processes a child's personal data. Recital 38 GDPR ("Children merit specific protection...") applies regardless of lawful basis. The two genuinely exposed areas are (a) the PUBLIC GALLERY exposing first name + age to signed-out visitors, which cannot be contract-necessary and needs its own separate, opt-in, informed consent from the account-holding adult — and where a very recent Garante provvedimento (29 April 2026) held that publishing images of under-14s online requires the prior consent of BOTH parents, expressly rejecting "affective purpose", small volume and private settings as defences; and (b) the optional child PHOTO sent to Together AI, which is optional, not objectively necessary, and therefore also a consent (not contract) processing operation.

On age verification: there is currently NO general Italian age-assurance obligation that catches this app. The only enacted Italian age-verification mandate (art. 13-bis d.l. 123/2023 conv. l. 159/2023, implemented by AGCOM delibera 96/25/CONS) is limited by its own Art. 1 to website operators and video-sharing platforms distributing pornographic images and video in Italy. The EDPB in Statement 1/2025 says explicitly that age-checking all users of content "suitable for all audiences and devoid of any risk" would FAIL the necessity and proportionality tests — so bolting on age verification would itself be a compliance error here. A Senate bill (DDL 1136) would set 15 for social/VSP accounts and raise the digital consent age to 15, but I could not verify it has been enacted.

Separately and practically: Apple App Review Guideline 1.3 says Kids Category apps "may not send personally identifiable information or device information to third parties." Sending a child's photo to Together AI is squarely that. If this app is (or is intended to be) in the Kids Category, that is a direct conflict independent of GDPR.

COPPA: does not apply to an Italy-only launch. FTC FAQ B.7 ties it to services "directed to children in the United States, or ... knowingly collect personal information from children in the U.S." It re-engages the moment the App Store listing or web signup is available to US users.

### Hard requirements

**What does GDPR Article 8 set as the default age for a child to consent to information society services, and what range may Member States derogate to?**

Art. 8(1) GDPR, verbatim: "Where point (a) of Article 6(1) applies, in relation to the offer of information society services directly to a child, the processing of the personal data of a child shall be lawful where the child is at least 16 years old. Where the child is below the age of 16 years, such processing shall be lawful only if and to the extent that consent is given or authorised by the holder of parental responsibility over the child. Member States may provide by law for a lower age for those purposes provided that such lower age is not below 13 years." So: DEFAULT 16; derogation range is 13-16 inclusive; 13 is the absolute floor. Art. 8(2): "The controller shall make reasonable efforts to verify in such cases that consent is given or authorised by the holder of parental responsibility over the child, taking into consideration available technology." Note the standard is "reasonable efforts", not certainty. Art. 8(3): "Paragraph 1 shall not affect the general contract law of Member States such as the rules on the validity, formation or effect of a contract in relation to a child." Retrieved 16 September 2026.

**Does COPPA (US) apply to an Italian launch, and does it apply to this app at all?**

No for an Italy-only launch; yes the moment US children are in scope. FTC 'Complying with COPPA: Frequently Asked Questions', FAQ B.7, verbatim: "The Internet is a global medium. Do websites and online services developed and run abroad have to comply with the Rule?" — "Foreign-based websites and online services must comply with COPPA if they are directed to children in the United States, or if they knowingly collect personal information from children in the U.S. The law's definition of 'operator' includes foreign-based websites and online services that are involved in commerce in the United States or its territories. As a related matter, U.S.-based sites and services that collect information from foreign children also are subject to COPPA." The trigger is the location of the CHILDREN, not of the company. COPPA's substantive scope: operators of services "directed to children under 13 years of age", or with "actual knowledge that they are collecting personal information online from a child under 13"; 'directed to children' is assessed on the multi-factor test in 16 C.F.R. § 312.2 (subject matter, visual content, animated characters, music, language, audience evidence, stated intended audience). Retrieved 16 September 2026.

**Is there any Italian requirement for AGE VERIFICATION or age assurance for a service like this?**

No — not for this app, on currently enacted law. Italy's only enacted age-verification mandate is art. 13-bis of d.l. 15 settembre 2023 n. 123, converted with modifications by legge 13 novembre 2023 n. 159, implemented by AGCOM delibera 96/25/CONS. Allegato A, Art. 1(1) defines the scope verbatim: "Il presente provvedimento, adottato in attuazione dell'art. 13-bis, comma 3, del Decreto, si applica ai gestori di siti web e dalle piattaforme di condivisione di video che diffondono in Italia immagini e video a carattere pornografico, stabiliti in Italia o stabiliti in un altro Stato membro." The title confirms the same limit. Obliged parties are listed by AGCOM and notified to the Commission. Deadlines: 12 November 2025 for operators established in Italy or outside the EU; 1 February 2026 for operators established in other Member States; AGCOM ordered its first blocking measures in March 2026. Art. 1(4) of Allegato A says the same methods are "altamente raccomandate" for other operators and other categories of content harmful to minors — a RECOMMENDATION, not an obligation. Crucially, the EDPB warns against over-applying age checks: Statement 1/2025 on Age Assurance (adopted 11 February 2025), para. 14: "a service provider processing personal data to check the age of all their users when accessing all their content or services, even when the content or services are suitable for all audiences and devoid of any risk, would not pass the necessity and proportionality tests." Para. 13 adds that safety measures "should always be the least intrusive of those available" and that age assurance often triggers a mandatory DPIA. Pending, NOT law as far as I could verify: Senate DDL S.1136 'Disposizioni per la tutela dei minori nella dimensione digitale', which would bar under-15s from social network and video-sharing accounts and would raise the digital consent age from 14 to 15, with verification via a national 'mini-wallet'. I researched the Italian/EU consumer withdrawal regime as it applies to My Book Lab's made-to-order printed books, using primary sources retrieved on 16 September 2026: Directive 2011/83/EU (EUR-Lex), the Codice del Consumo (D.Lgs. 206/2005) via Gazzetta Ufficiale, the Commission's Guidance Notice 2021/C 525/01 and its 2014 DG Justice predecessor, CJEU judgments C-529/19 (Möbel Kraft) and C-208/19 (NK), AGCM decision no. 26820 of 25 October 2017 (PS10846 Zucchetti Poltrone & Divani, Bollettino 43/2017), and Directive (EU) 2019/771 with the Codice del Consumo articles as amended by D.Lgs. 170/2021.

Headline answer, in three parts.

(1) The exemption almost certainly exists for the printed book. Art. 16(c) of Directive 2011/83/EU and art. 59, comma 1, lett. c) of the Codice del Consumo exclude the 14-day withdrawal right for "la fornitura di beni confezionati su misura o chiaramente personalizzati". A book printed to order containing a specific child's own story, own illustrations and own first name is unique, produced to the consumer's individual specifications, and unsaleable to anyone else — which is precisely the ratio the AGCM identified at §§51-52 of the Zucchetti decision. The 2014 Commission guidance expressly listed "T-shirts with a personalised print" as covered. Möbel Kraft (C-529/19) confirms the exclusion applies from the moment of contracting, irrespective of whether production has begun.

(2) But the current Terms wording does NOT properly claim it, and that is the real exposure. "Orders cannot be cancelled once printing has begun" is a contractual cancellation policy, not the statutory exclusion. It (a) fails to give the pre-contractual information required by art. 6(1)(k) of the Directive / art. 49, comma 1, lett. m) of the Codice del Consumo that the consumer has NO right of withdrawal; (b) makes the loss of the right depend on the state of progress of production — the exact construction the CJEU rejected in Möbel Kraft; and (c) by implying a cancellation right exists before printing, it invites the reading that an ordinary withdrawal right exists and was then cut short. AGCM footnote 22 of the Zucchetti decision is squarely on point: a correctly-worded art. 59(1)(c) clause, when buried in small print in a general terms paragraph, was held inadequate to inform the consumer clearly and comprehensibly. GDL was fined EUR 150,000.

(3) The reprint-or-refund promise is a reasonable commercial gesture but is NOT by itself an adequate statement of the statutory guarantee. Italian law gives 2 years of seller liability for non-conformity (art. 133 cod. cons.), a 26-month prescription period, a 1-year presumption that the defect pre-existed (art. 135), a consumer-chosen repair/replacement remedy free of charge (artt. 135-bis, 135-ter), and price reduction/termination as fallbacks. Any clause that narrows this is void. "Damaged" is also too narrow a trigger — non-conformity covers wrong binding, missing/duplicated pages, colour failure, wrong content, and anything not matching what was ordered.

The concrete fix is narrow and cheap: move a clear "no right of withdrawal, because art. 59(1)(c)" notice to the point of order (next to the "ordine con obbligo di pagare" button), repeat it in the durable-medium order confirmation, delete the "once printing has begun" framing, and restate the 2-year legal guarantee as a floor with reprint-or-refund sitting on top of it.

**What is the general right of withdrawal (diritto di recesso) period for distance contracts under EU law and the Italian Codice del Consumo?**

14 calendar days, without giving any reason. Directive 2011/83/EU, Art. 9(1): "the consumer shall have a period of 14 days to withdraw from a distance or off-premises contract, without giving any reason". Art. 9(2)(b) sets the start point for sales contracts as the day on which the consumer (or a third party other than the carrier indicated by the consumer) acquires physical possession of the goods. Italy transposes this at art. 52 Codice del Consumo (D.Lgs. 206/2005, as amended by D.Lgs. 21/2014): comma 1 — "il consumatore dispone di un periodo di quattordici giorni per recedere da un contratto a distanza o negoziato fuori dei locali commerciali"; comma 2 — the period runs from acquisition of physical possession of the goods for sales contracts. A 30-day variant exists only for unsolicited home visits and organised excursions (art. 52, comma 1-bis), which is irrelevant to an app/web sale. Retrieved 16 Sep 2026.

**Does the exemption depend on whether printing has already started?**

No — and this is dispositive against the current wording. CJEU judgment of 21 October 2020 in Case C-529/19, Möbel Kraft GmbH & Co. The Court held the exception is inherent in the subject matter of the contract and "applies from the outset to that consumer, without being conditional on the occurrence of such an event and irrespective of whether that contract has been performed". The Commission Notice §5.11.2 restates this: "where the exception applies, the restriction on withdrawal from contracts for the supply of goods made to the consumer's specifications or clearly personalised is absolute and does not depend on whether the trader has started working on the order or not", adding that "the existence of the consumer's right of withdrawal would be at issue if it depended on the state of progress of the performance of the contract by the trader, a state of progress of which the consumer is not, as a general rule, informed and over which he or she has, a fortiori, no control". **What are the rules on conformity/defects for physical goods sold to Italian consumers, and what is the guarantee period?**

Art. 133, comma 3: "L'azione diretta a far valere i difetti non dolosamente occultati dal venditore si prescrive, in ogni caso, nel termine di ventisei mesi dalla consegna del bene." Art. 135 (onere della prova): any lack of conformity appearing within ONE year of delivery is presumed to have existed at delivery unless incompatible with the nature of the goods or the defect (Directive Art. 11(1)). **Is the current 'reprint or refund for damaged books' promise adequate?**

It is a good commercial gesture but is not, as described, an adequate statement of the statutory position, for five sourced reasons. (1) TRIGGER TOO NARROW. The statutory trigger is 'difetto di conformità' — non-conformity with the subjective and objective requirements of the contract — not 'damage'. Missing or duplicated pages, a misbound spine, colour that does not match, the wrong child's name, the wrong story, poor print quality, wrong trim size, or a book that simply is not what was ordered are all non-conformities even though nothing is 'damaged'. (2) DURATION. The remedy must be available for two years from delivery (art. 133, comma 1), with a 26-month prescription limit (comma 3). Any short reporting window — 14 days, 30 days, 'on receipt' — cannot cut off the statutory right; under art. 135-sexies and Directive (EU) 2019/771 Art. 21(1) such a limitation is not binding on the consumer. D.Lgs. 170/2021 removed even the old two-month notification duty. (3) BURDEN OF PROOF. For the first year the company cannot demand that the consumer prove the defect existed at delivery — art. 135 presumes it did. Photo-evidence requirements are fine as a service workflow, but must not be stated as a precondition to the legal remedy. (4) WHO CHOOSES. Art. 135-bis, comma 2 gives the consumer the choice between repair and replacement; presenting reprint-or-refund as the company's choice is a misstatement, though in practice reprint will usually be the only feasible restoration remedy for a book. And the refund route is not purely discretionary: under comma 4 the consumer is ENTITLED to termination and refund where the seller has not completed the remedy, the defect persists, or the seller refuses. (5) FRAMING. Under art. 135-sexies the promise must be presented as ADDITIONAL to the legal guarantee, never as a substitute for it. A voluntary commercial guarantee is separately binding on its own stated terms (the D.Lgs. 170/2021 regime on garanzia convenzionale), so a generous promise is safe to make — but it must sit on top of the statutory floor, not replace it. I researched Italian and EU rules on auto-renewing subscriptions, in-app purchases and virtual currency sold to consumers, working from primary sources: Normattiva for the Codice del Consumo (artt. 26, 49, 51, 52, 57, 59), EUR-Lex for Directive 2011/83/EU and for the Commission's UCPD Guidance (Notice 2021/C 526/01), the European Commission's hosting of the CPC Network's Key Principles on In-game Virtual Currencies (21 March 2025) and the CPC Common Position on in-app purchases (18 July 2014), four AGCM decisions and press releases (PS8754 Gameloft/iTunes/Google/Amazon 2015; PS11594 Electronic Arts Provv. 28368; PS11595 Activision Blizzard Provv. 28452; PS13020/PS13039 opened 16 January 2026), and Apple's own published Media Services Terms (Italy), Developer Program Schedule 2 (v126, 17 Dec 2025), App Review Guidelines and subscriptions documentation. All retrieved 16 September 2026.\n\nHeadline answers. (1) Art. 49 c.1 cod. cons. sets the pre-contractual list; for a subscription the load-bearing items are lett. e) (total price per billing period), q) (duration and, for auto-renewing contracts, the conditions for cancelling) and r) (minimum duration of the consumer's obligations). (2) Art. 51 c.2 requires the five items a), e), n-bis), q), r) immediately before the order and a button reading 'ordine con obbligo di pagare' or an unambiguous equivalent; the sanction is that the consumer is NOT BOUND. (3) Art. 59 c.1 lett. o) removes the 14-day right for digital content only if THREE cumulative conditions are met — prior express consent, express acknowledgement of losing the right, and confirmation on a durable medium; art. 57 c.4 lett. b) says that if any fails, the consumer owes nothing for what was supplied. (4) On coins: there is no bespoke statute, but the CPC's March 2025 Key Principles are the operative text and they are unusually specific — euro prices must be shown alongside coin prices at the undiscounted rate; bundle sizes must not force over-purchase; and, most consequentially for My Book Lab, virtual currency is expressly stated NOT to be digital content, so the 14-day withdrawal right applies to unused coins and a blanket non-refundable clause is named as a practice to avoid. (5) AGCM's position on minors is expressed through enforcement, and it is escalating: it has pursued the DEVELOPER as well as the store since 2014, it accepted from EA a commitment to default the spending limit to zero euro changeable only by a deliberate parental act, and on 16 January 2026 it opened two investigations into Activision Blizzard explicitly attacking parental-control defaults that leave purchasing enabled and practices that obscure the real value of virtual currency for minors. (7) Apple handles payment, VAT invoicing to the consumer, the 14-day withdrawal channel, refunds, renewal/cancellation UI and Ask to Buy; the developer retains the in-app subscription disclosures (Schedule 2 §3.8(b)), the EULA, all warranty and support claims (§6.1-6.2), reimbursement of refunds (§6.3), and — decisively under Italian law — responsibility for its own commercial practices as a 'professionista' under art. 18 lett. b) cod. cons.\n\nTwo things I could not verify and that matter: whether Apple's actual Italian IAP sheet carries a compliant pay-obligation label, and whether Italy sits in Apple's agent or commissionaire exhibit. ### Hard requirements

**1. What pre-contractual information must be given to an Italian consumer before they enter an auto-renewing subscription (distance contract)?**

Art. 49, comma 1, D.Lgs. 206/2005 (Codice del Consumo) requires the trader, BEFORE the consumer is bound, to give — in a clear and comprehensible way — a long mandatory list. Verbatim items I retrieved from Normattiva that matter most here: a) "le caratteristiche principali dei beni o servizi"; b) "l'identità del professionista"; c) geographic address, telephone and electronic address of the trader; e) "il prezzo totale dei beni o dei servizi comprensivo delle imposte", and specifically "nel caso di un contratto a tempo indeterminato o di un contratto comprendente un abbonamento, il prezzo totale include i costi totali per periodo di fatturazione"; e-bis) if applicable, that the price was personalised by automated decision-making; g) payment, delivery and performance arrangements; h) conditions, time limits and procedures for exercising withdrawal plus the model withdrawal form; m) information that the withdrawal right does not apply (or the circumstances in which it is lost); n-ter) reminder of the legal guarantee of conformity for digital content and services; q) "la durata del contratto, se applicabile, o, se il contratto è a tempo indeterminato o è un contratto a rinnovo automatico, le condizioni per recedere dal contratto"; r) "se applicabile, la durata minima degli obblighi del consumatore a norma del contratto"; s) deposits/financial guarantees; t)-u) functionality, compatibility and interoperability of digital content/services; v) extra-judicial complaint and redress mechanisms. Art. 49 also provides that these items form an integral part of the contract and that the burden of proving compliance is on the trader. The EU source is Art. 6 of Directive 2011/83/EU (Consumer Rights Directive), consolidated version.

**2. What are the rules on the 'order with obligation to pay' button for distance contracts concluded electronically? Cite the article.**

Art. 51, comma 2, Codice del Consumo (verbatim, retrieved from Normattiva): "Se un contratto a distanza che deve essere concluso con mezzi elettronici impone al consumatore l'obbligo di pagare, l'operatore economico gli comunica in modo chiaro ed evidente le informazioni di cui all'articolo 49, comma 1, lettere a), e), n-bis), q) e r), direttamente prima che il consumatore inoltri l'ordine. **6. Unfair commercial practices directed at children: what does the Codice del Consumo say about direct exhortations to children to buy? Is a coin store shown to a 6-12 year old at risk, and what mitigations exist?**

Art. 26 cod. cons. lists practices "considerate in ogni caso aggressive" — i.e. unfair per se, with no need to prove distortion of economic behaviour. Lettera e) prohibits: "includere in un messaggio pubblicitario un'esortazione diretta ai bambini affinché acquistino o convincano i genitori o altri adulti ad acquistare loro i prodotti reclamizzati". This is the transposition of Annex I point 28 UCPD: "Including in an advertisement a direct exhortation to children to buy advertised products or persuade their parents or other adults to buy advertised products for them." That AGCM reads lettera e) as the children's-exhortation provision is confirmed by its own decisions, which cite "art. 26, lettere c) ed e)" for that conduct (PS8754) and "26, lettera e)" (PS11594, PS11595). The Commission's UCPD Guidance §3.7 says the ban "includes putting pressure on children to buy a product directly or to persuade adults to buy items for them ('pester power')", that the assessment turns on "the design of the marketing, the medium used ..., the type of language used, the presence of topics or characters that may in particular appeal to children, the presence of age-restrictions, providing direct links to making purchases", that "A national enforcement authority or court is also not bound by the trader's own definition of the target group", and that "The assessment should also take into account the steps that the trader has taken in protecting minors from direct exhortation. Traders should adapt the marketing according to the consumers that could actually be reached by the practice, not only according to the intended target group." Crucially, §3.7 and §4.2.9 record the CPC position that "point No 28 Annex I to the UCPD applies to games that are likely to appeal to children, not only those solely or specifically targeted at children". The CPC 2025 Key Principles (Principle 7) restate this and add the action point: "Do not use commercial practices that directly exhort children to buy in-game virtual currencies or digital content or services, or persuade adults to buy it for them or on their behalf", and "Ensure that functional parental controls with age-appropriate default settings are provided, such as by default disabling the possibility of spending real-world money ... if the video game is not exclusively limited to an adult audience only". But the same document warns explicitly: "age gates and parental control functions do not limit traders' responsibilities to adapt their practices to the players."

**What is the mandatory content of a privacy notice under GDPR Arts. 13-14?**

Art. 13 (data collected from the data subject) requires: (a) identity and contact details of the controller and, where applicable, its representative; (b) contact details of the DPO, where applicable; (c) purposes AND legal basis for each processing; (d) the legitimate interests pursued, where Art. 6(1)(f) is relied on; (e) recipients or categories of recipients; (f) the fact of any transfer to a third country, the existence or absence of an adequacy decision, the Art. 46/47/49 safeguard relied on, and how to obtain a copy. Art. 13(2) adds: (a) storage period or the criteria to determine it; (b) the rights of access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability and objection; (c) the right to withdraw consent, where consent is the basis; (d) the right to lodge a complaint with a supervisory authority; (e) whether providing the data is a statutory or contractual requirement and the consequences of not providing it; (f) the existence of automated decision-making/profiling under Art. 22 with meaningful information about the logic and consequences. Art. 13(3) requires fresh information before any further purpose. Art. 14 applies where the data were NOT obtained from the data subject and additionally requires (1)(d) the categories of personal data concerned and (2)(f) the source of the data, with a deadline of one month / first communication.

COMMONLY MISSED, ranked by how often the Garante actually catches them: (i) the LEGAL BASIS stated granularly per processing activity, not as one blanket sentence — this was the first substantive failing in the Replika decision; (ii) RETENTION periods or the criteria to determine them — the Garante's 2025 injunction against Luka lists this as an outstanding breach; (iii) naming the actual RECIPIENTS rather than vague categories; (iv) naming the specific THIRD COUNTRIES and the specific Art. 46 mechanism; (v) the Art. 27 representative's identity where the controller is outside the EU; (vi) the Art. 22 section describing rights that do not in fact exist — the Garante treated misleading Art. 22 language as itself a transparency breach.

**Is a child's PHOTO sent to a third-party AI biometric/special category data under Art. 9, or ordinary personal data?**

ORDINARY personal data, on these facts. Art. 4(14) defines biometric data as "personal data resulting from SPECIFIC TECHNICAL PROCESSING relating to the physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics of a natural person, WHICH ALLOW OR CONFIRM THE UNIQUE IDENTIFICATION of that natural person, such as facial images or dactyloscopic data". Art. 9(1) prohibits, among others, "biometric data FOR THE PURPOSE OF UNIQUELY IDENTIFYING a natural person" — so the purpose element is on the face of the prohibition. Recital 51 is explicit and directly on point: "The processing of photographs should not systematically be considered to be processing of special categories of personal data as they are covered by the definition of biometric data only when processed through a specific technical means allowing the unique identification or authentication of a natural person." EDPB Guidelines 3/2019 para 74: "To qualify as biometric data as defined in the GDPR, processing of raw data... must imply a MEASUREMENT of this characteristics... The video footage of an individual cannot however in itself be considered as biometric data under Article 9, if it has not been specifically technically processed in order to contribute to the identification of an individual." Para 76 sets three cumulative criteria: nature of the data; means and way of processing ("resulting from a specific technical processing"); and purpose ("data must be used for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person"). Para 80 closes the loop: "when the purpose of the processing is for example to distinguish one category of people from another but not to uniquely identify anyone the processing does not fall under Article 9." Para 82 confirms Art. 9 DOES apply where the controller stores biometric templates in order to uniquely identify a person.

ONE CAVEAT. A photo can still reveal Art. 9 data by its content — e.g. racial or ethnic origin, or a visible health condition. The EDPB in Guidelines 3/2019 para 70 takes a restrained line on this: "Video footage showing a data subject wearing glasses or using a wheel chair are not per se considered to be special categories of personal data" — but political opinions inferable from an image of a person at a protest would fall under Art. 9.

**What must be disclosed to users about AI-generated content, and is machine-readable marking (watermarking) required of someone using another party's model?**

Art. 50 imposes four distinct duties. (1) Art. 50(1) — providers must design systems intended to interact directly with natural persons so that those persons are informed they are interacting with an AI system, "unless this is obvious from the point of view of a natural person who is reasonably well-informed, observant and circumspect". (2) Art. 50(2) — "Providers of AI systems, including general-purpose AI systems, generating synthetic audio, image, video or text content, shall ensure that the outputs of the AI system are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated", with technical solutions that are "effective, interoperable, robust and reliable as far as this is technically feasible"; it does not apply where the system performs an assistive function for standard editing or does not substantially alter the input data or its semantics. (3) Art. 50(4) — deployers of a system generating or manipulating image, audio or video content constituting a deep fake must disclose that the content has been artificially generated or manipulated; where the content is part of "an evidently artistic, creative, satirical, fictional or analogous work", the duty is limited to disclosure "in an appropriate manner that does not hamper the display or enjoyment of the work". (4) Art. 50(5) — the information must be given "in a clear and distinguishable manner at the latest at the time of the first interaction or exposure" and must "conform to the applicable accessibility requirements". Recital 133 lists acceptable techniques: "watermarks, metadata identifications, cryptographic methods for proving provenance and authenticity of content, logging methods, fingerprints or other techniques". So machine-readable marking is a PROVIDER obligation, and a downstream integrator who ships the system under its own name is that provider. Application date: 2 August 2026 (Art. 113), with a narrow transitional in new Art. 111(4) inserted by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744: providers of generative AI systems "placed on the market before 2 August 2026 shall take the necessary steps in order to comply with Article 50(2) by 2 December 2026". Penalties for Art. 50 breaches: up to EUR 15,000,000 or 3% of worldwide annual turnover (Art. 99(4)(g)), reduced for SMEs to the lower of the two figures (Art. 99(6)). Retrieved 16 September 2026.

Unsafe side (efficacy/health claims creating a medical intended purpose): 'helps children with dyslexia read better', 'reduces ADHD symptoms', 'improves attention', 'therapeutic', 'clinically proven', 'recommended by specialists for ADHD'. Crossing the line does not just risk an AGCM fine — it can qualify the software as a medical device requiring CE marking under the MDR, which the company is plainly not set up for.

**Action:** Write an explicit claims policy with a banned-words list (cura, terapeutico, tratta, migliora la dislessia/ADHD, clinicamente provato, riduce i sintomi) and an allowed-claims list phrased as product features. Apply it to the app store listing, the website, the Instagram campaign, creator briefs and school-facing sales decks. Have a regulatory lawyer review the final claim set against MDR art. 2(1)/2(12) before launch — this is the one topic where getting it wrong changes the regulatory category of the product.

**What does EAA conformity mean in practice, and is there a microenterprise exemption?**

There is a full exemption for microenterprises providing services. Directive (EU) 2019/882 Art. 4(5): "Microenterprises providing services shall be exempt from complying with the accessibility requirements referred to in paragraph 3 of this Article and any obligations relating to the compliance with those requirements." Art. 3(23) defines a microenterprise as "an enterprise which employs fewer than 10 persons and which has an annual turnover not exceeding EUR 2 million or an annual balance sheet total not exceeding EUR 2 million". Recital 70 explains that requiring even a proportionality assessment of microenterprises "would in itself constitute a disproportionate burden". Italy mirrors this: D.Lgs. 82/2022 art. 2 lett. bb) defines "microimpresa" identically and art. 3 comma 3 provides "Le microimprese che forniscono servizi sono esentate dall'osservanza dei requisiti di accessibilità di cui al comma 2"; art. 24 comma 1 excludes from sanction the cases of exemption under art. 3 comma 3. For non-exempt operators, the substantive content is Annex I Section III (making "websites, including the related online applications, and mobile device-based services, including mobile applications, accessible in a consistent and adequate way by making them perceivable, operable, understandable and robust", plus information provided via more than one sensory channel, adequate font size and contrast, adjustable letter/line/paragraph spacing, text alternatives for non-text content) and Section IV. Art. 13 D.Lgs. 82/2022 (EAA Art. 14) allows a fundamental-alteration / disproportionate-burden defence, but it must be assessed against the Allegato V criteria, documented, and retained for at least five years, with a copy produced on request. Service providers must also publish information explaining how the service meets the requirements (EAA Art. 13(2)). Retrieved 16 September 2026.

**Do the DSA's minor-protection advertising rules affect the Instagram campaign or the public gallery?**

Article 28 of Regulation (EU) 2022/2065 (DSA) provides at paragraph 1 that "Providers of online platforms accessible to minors shall put in place appropriate and proportionate measures to ensure a high level of privacy, safety, and security of minors, on their service", and at paragraph 2 that "Providers of online platform shall not present advertisements on their interface based on profiling as defined in Article 4, point (4), of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 using personal data of the recipient of the service when they are aware with reasonable certainty that the recipient of the service is a minor." Paragraph 3 clarifies this does not oblige platforms to process additional personal data to determine whether a recipient is a minor. The obligation is addressed to platform providers, not to advertisers. Retrieved 16 September 2026.
