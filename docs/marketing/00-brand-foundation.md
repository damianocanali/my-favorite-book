## Positioning — Parents

**Who:** **Primary buyer: the "my kid has stories in him but won't put them on paper" parent.**

Demographics: 32-48, mother in ~80% of cases, one or more children aged 6-12, household income $60-150k, US/UK/CA/AU. Owns an iPad that is currently mostly a YouTube and Roblox device, which she feels bad about. She is the household's designated "school worrier" — she's the one who sat in the parent-teacher conference and the one who filled in the IEP/504 or EHCP paperwork.

The sharpest segment inside this: **the parent of a 7-10 year old with an ADHD, dyslexia, or dysgraphia diagnosis (or a strong suspicion of one) whose child is verbally way ahead of what they'll write down.** This gap is the single most emotionally loaded thing in her life right now. Her child tells elaborate, funny, structured stories out loud at dinner and then produces four labored sentences for a school writing task and melts down. She has been told her child is "bright but not applying himself." She knows that's wrong and she cannot prove it.

Secondary, smaller but high-converting: **secular and eclectic homeschoolers** (not classical/Charlotte Mason purists — they'll object to the screen). They buy curriculum supplements, already pay for 3-5 subscriptions, and they need a writing component their kid doesn't fight them on. They convert fast and churn less.

Where they are on Instagram — she follows a stack roughly like this:
- Neurodivergent parenting: @theadhd.couple, @drhayleywatson, @understood.org, @adhd_love, @the.dyslexia.classroom, @thedyslexicevolution, @connectionsinmind. Reels captioned "if your kid says 'I don't know what to write', they're not being lazy — this is what's actually happening."
- Gentle/respectful parenting: @drbeckyatgoodinside (the single biggest signal account), @biglittlefeelings, @curious.parenting, @janetlansbury. She has absorbed the vocabulary: co-regulation, capable kid, connection before correction. Copy that shames the kid or the parent is instantly disqualifying.
- Kids' activities & screen-time-guilt relief: @busytoddler (aged up), @daysw.grey, @happilyevermom, plus the anti-screen-time voices @screensandteens and Jonathan Haidt / Anxious Generation content that she half-agrees with and feels judged by.
- Homeschool: @homeschool.thehappyway, @wildandfreechildren, @blossomandroot.
- Teacher-adjacent literacy accounts on the science-of-reading side: @thescienceofreading, @tootiredteacher.

Behavioral tells that she's the buyer, not a browser: she searches "creative writing app for dyslexic child," "how to help my ADHD child start writing," "is it bad if my kid uses AI for schoolwork." She reads App Store reviews before downloading — specifically the 2- and 3-star ones. She has been burned by at least two "educational" apps that turned out to be a skin over a rewards-and-gems loop, and she will open the app herself before she hands it to her child.

Buying trigger moments, in order of strength: (1) a school report or conference that flags writing; (2) the start of a school holiday she needs to fill without guilt; (3) a homework meltdown that ended in tears at 8pm; (4) a birthday or Christmas where she wants the gift to be the printed book.

**Pains**

- Her child can TELL a brilliant story and cannot WRITE one. The gap between verbal ability and output on the page is the wound. She has watched a kid who narrates a 20-minute saga about a dragon in the car produce three sentences for a school assignment and then say 'I'm bad at writing.'
- The blank page triggers a shutdown. It's not reluctance, it's a genuine stall — her child sits with a pencil for 25 minutes producing nothing, and every prompt she offers ('just write anything!') makes it worse. She's out of ways to help without doing it for him.
- Handwriting itself is the bottleneck for a dysgraphic or dyslexic kid. The physical act costs so much working memory that the story evaporates before it reaches the paper. She suspects this but has no tool that separates 'having ideas' from 'forming letters.'
- Her child has decided he is 'bad at writing' and it's calcifying into identity by age 8. She is watching a self-concept close, and that scares her more than the grade does.
- Screen time guilt with no good alternative. She knows the iPad is 90% consumption. Every 'educational' app she's tried is a dopamine slot machine with a worksheet bolted on, and she can't tell the difference from the App Store page.
- She cannot evaluate AI claims and is tired of being marketed to about it. She's seen 'AI-powered learning' on everything and assumes it means the machine does the work. She has genuinely no way to find out what a given app's AI actually does before she buys.
- A real, specific fear: that a tool meant to help will teach her child to outsource thinking before he's ever had the experience of finishing something hard himself. She'd rather he struggle than be handed a paragraph.
- Nothing her child makes is permanent. Drawings go in the recycling, school work comes home in a crumpled folder in July. There's no artifact, nothing that says 'you made this and it mattered.'
- Reward systems that hijack her kid. She's watched streaks and coins turn a calm child into someone panicking about losing a number, and she's now suspicious of any app with a fire emoji in it.
- Sessions that never end. Apps with no natural stopping point mean every single session ends in a fight over the iPad, so she pre-emptively doesn't start them.

**Messaging pillars

**Differentiators

- Sentence starters are position-aware — src/lib/sentenceStarters.js keeps separate opening, middle, and ending banks, so a student stuck on page 5 gets "Just when things seemed hopeless," not another story opener. Most writing tools serve one undifferentiated prompt list.
- The interface itself changes with the author's age rather than just the reading level of the content: at 7 and under the app enlarges type and touch targets and caps a book at 12 pages and 100 characters per page (useAgeAdaptive.js) — a built-in ceiling on task length that matters more for a distractible writer than any prompt.
- A genuine zero-typing path to a finished book: Story Blanks builds a complete story from tapped word-bank choices, and the word banks were deliberately kept concrete and picturable — a design note in src/data/storyTemplates.js explains that "purple" makes a better illustration prompt than "shimmering."
- Class submission requires no student login at the moment of submitting (api/classroom-submit.js is unauthenticated) — a six-character code on the whiteboard replaces a roster, an invite link, and an IT ticket.
- The class gallery is deliberately text-first — illustrations are stripped on submission — which for once favors the teacher: you read 28 students' writing without 28 AI pictures competing for attention.
- The AI's default posture is interrogative: two of three Story Buddy modes return questions or one-line starters and cannot produce a paragraph (api/story-buddy.js), and the iOS app ships only those two — the paragraph writer does not exist in the native app at all.
- It is a browser app for the teacher side, which for a Chromebook school is an advantage, not a compromise — nothing to install, nothing to get approved.

**Voice**

TONE RULES. This audience has been marketed to badly for a decade and detects a pitch in one sentence. They respond to: specificity (a named grade, a named minute of the day, a real number), classroom proof (student work, a screen recording of the actual tool, a teacher's own hands), constraints stated up front (what it does NOT do), time saved expressed concretely, and being addressed as a professional who makes instructional decisions. Say "students," not "kids," in educator posts — "kids" reads parent-facing. Say "supports" and "scaffolds," not "features." Say "low floor" and "entry point," not "easy." Use UDL vocabulary correctly and sparingly — multiple means of representation, action and expression, engagement — and never claim compliance or conformance with anything.

WHAT MAKES THEM BOUNCE INSTANTLY: exclamation-mark stacking, "revolutionary" / "game-changer" / "transform your classroom," any implication that they are not already doing enough for their students, teacher-savior imagery, "AI-powered" as a headline, stock photos of tidy children at desks, a CTA that says "download now," countdown-timer urgency, and — fatally with this crowd — a standards or compliance claim you cannot document.

TWO HARD CONTENT RULES DERIVED FROM THE CODE. (1) Never write "WCAG compliant," "UDL-aligned," "meets COGA," or any standards-alignment claim. The landing page asserts the app "follows Universal Design for Learning (UDL) principles and WCAG/COGA accessibility guidelines," but there is no audit, no VPAT, no conformance artifact, and no standards mapping anywhere in the repository, and no Common Core or state-standards alignment exists in any file. What is defensible is describing the design choices themselves — multiple means of expression through typing, dictation, or tapping word banks — and letting the educator judge them against her own framework. (2) Never say "high contrast mode" or "tracks student writing progress." Both appear on the marketing landing page; neither exists in the code.

SAMPLE SENTENCES IN VOICE:
1. "Three helpers live in the writing screen. Two of them will only ask questions or hand you one opening line. The third writes a paragraph, and the button says 'Write for Me' — so you can decide as a class whether that one is allowed."
2. "The class code is six characters with no O, no zero, no I and no one — because you are going to read it out loud to twenty-eight second graders."
3. "Fair warning before you try it with a class: the teacher dashboard is web only, illustrations are stripped from submitted books so you get the writing, and your class list lives in whichever browser you made it in. Write the code down."

ANTI-EXAMPLES — never publish anything like these:
1. "Transform your classroom with AI-powered, UDL-aligned, WCAG-compliant creative writing! Track every student's progress in real time! 🚀✨" (Four claims, three of them false: no progress tracking, no conformance evidence, and the AI-first framing is the exact thing this audience screenshots to mock.)
2. "Finally, an app that makes writing easy for your struggling learners — just download it from the App Store and watch their confidence soar." (Deficit framing of students, "easy" instead of "accessible," and it sends an educator to an App Store app that contains no teacher surface at all.)

**Objections**

- **“"So the AI just writes the story for them. That's not writing."”** → Partly true and worth being precise about. Story Buddy has three modes (api/story-buddy.js). Two of them cannot write your student's story — 'starters' returns three one-sentence openers to continue from, and 'questions' returns three questions to think with. The third, labeled 'Write for Me' in the web editor, does generate a 2-4 sentence paragraph. There is no teacher toggle to switch it off, and I will not pretend otherwise. Two mitigations that are real: the native iOS app ships only the starters and questions modes — the paragraph writer does not exist there — and on Free the AI helper is capped at 3 uses per day, which is a natural ceiling for a trial. The honest recommendation is to treat 'Write for Me' the way you treat a thesaurus or a sentence-frame poster: name the rule in your class before you name the tool.
- **“"More screen time in my literacy block. My parents and my principal will push back."”** → The task has a defined end. A book for a student aged 7 or under is capped at 12 pages with 100 characters per page (useAgeAdaptive.js) — that is a bounded writing task, not an open feed. There is no video, no social feed, and no notification loop in the writing flow. And the output leaves the screen: the finished book prints to PDF on a paid plan and can be ordered as a physical copy. If the pushback is really about substance, the counter is that the child is composing text, not consuming it. What I will not tell you is that it reduces screen time — it does not, it replaces a paper task with a screen task.
- **“"Where does my students' writing go, and who can see it? I need to answer this for my admin."”** → Straight answer from the code. A submitted book is stored on the app's servers (Supabase) attached to the class code, with illustrations and cover images stripped out first (api/classroom-submit.js). The class gallery is readable by ANYONE who has the six-character code — the endpoint is deliberately public so students can reach it without a login, and it is rate-limited rather than authenticated. That means the code is the password: do not post it publicly, and do not reuse it across years. Two more things to weigh: a submitted book carries whatever the student typed as an author name, so tell them to use a first name or an initial; and there is currently no way for you to delete or edit a submission once it lands. Nothing here has been through a student-data privacy review (no signed DPA process, no FERPA or COPPA attestation flow exists in the product), so if your district requires a data agreement before students enter content, this does not clear that bar yet. For a small group, a resource room, or a homeschool co-op, the code-as-password model is usually acceptable; for a whole-district rollout it is not.
- **“"I don't have a budget and I'm not asking for a PO."”** → You would be paying personally — checkout is Stripe self-serve, and there is no invoicing, quote, or purchase-order path in the product. Teacher is $13.99/month or $109.99/year (about $9.17/month) with a 14-day free trial; Family is $6.99/month or $54.99/year. The Teacher plan buys YOUR account unlimited books, unlimited Story Buddy, 200 AI illustrations a day, PDF export, and the classroom dashboard. Be clear-eyed about what it does not buy: there are no student seats. Each student needs a free account to save a book, and Free is capped at one saved book total, three AI helper uses a day and two AI illustrations a day. So the realistic fit today is one book per student per term, a small group, or a co-op — not unlimited class-wide writing on your subscription. Anyone selling you a per-seat classroom license is not describing this product.
- **“"I downloaded the app and there's no teacher section."”** → Correct, and it is the single most important thing to say before someone tries. The App Store app has five tabs — Books, Gallery, Create, Orders, Account — and no teacher or classroom surface at all; a student using the iPad app cannot even submit to a class. Everything on the educator side lives at mybooklab.app in a browser: mybooklab.app/teacher to make a class, and a /classroom/CODE link to read what came in. That runs fine on a school Chromebook or laptop, which is where you probably are during a writing block anyway. The app is the student-and-family experience; the web is the classroom experience.
- **“"Is it actually accessible, or is that just marketing? I've been burned."”** → Here is exactly what exists, so you can judge it yourself. Shipping in the web app: an always-visible toolbar above the writing area with read-aloud (word-by-word highlighting, slowed to 0.85 speed), continuous voice dictation, an OpenDyslexic font toggle, and a focus mode; position-aware sentence starters and a word bank of feelings and actions; a visual progress map; an age-adaptive interface; and a tap-only fill-in-the-blank story path whose word banks were chosen to be keyboard- and VoiceOver-navigable. Not shipping, despite what the marketing page says: there is no high-contrast mode, and there is no classroom progress tracking. Also missing: no accessibility audit, no VPAT, no conformance statement, and no standards alignment of any kind. I would call this thoughtfully designed for access, and I would not call it certified — if your district requires documented conformance, this does not have it yet.
- **“"How much of my prep time does this actually cost me?"”** → Setup is genuinely small: sign in, type a class name, get a code — under two minutes, no roster, no invites. The costs are on the other side and you should know them before period one. Your class list is saved in the browser you created it in and there is no way to look up your classes from another device, so write the code down. Books arrive text-only, which is faster to read but means the illustrations your students made do not come with them. There is no feedback box, rubric, comment, grade, or bulk export — you read the books on screen. And submissions are limited to about 20 per hour from a single network, so a 30-student class period submitting at once will hit a wall; stagger it or split it across two periods.
- **“"Does it work on Chromebooks / does it need an install?"”** → Nothing to install — it is a website, which is the point for a Chromebook cart. One real caveat: the voice-input button uses the browser's speech recognition API, which works in Chrome and Edge and is absent in Firefox, so on a Chromebook you are fine and on a Firefox-standardized machine that one button will be greyed out. Read-aloud uses the browser's speech synthesis and is much more widely supported. The dyslexia font loads from a third-party font CDN, so if your district's filter is aggressive, test that toggle before you promise it to a student.

### Shooting rules

CHILD SAFETY — these are absolute and override every creative consideration.
1. No real children's faces. Ever. Not in a screen recording, not in a reflection on the device glass, not out of focus in the background, not a photo on a shelf behind the iPad, not in a testimonial graphic. If a child's hands appear operating the device, they must be unidentifiable — no faces, no distinctive marks.
2. No real names. Every name visible in the UI — author name, character name, book title, sign-in email, avatar label — must be fictional. The app asks "Who's the author of this story?" on the very first wizard step, so a name field WILL be on screen; type a demo name into it before you record.
3. No real school names, class names, teacher names, or town names, in the UI or in any dictated audio.
4. Demo content only. Use "Theo and the Star Bear" — the sample book already shipped in the app (Models/SampleBook.swift, author "Theo", age 7, characters Theo 👦🏼 and Twinkle 🌟, setting The Glowing Forest, 12 pages, illustrations at /sample-book/*.png). It is designed to be shown publicly. Never film a real user's bookshelf, a real account's books, or content pulled from the production database. If a shot needs a second book, author it fresh as demo content in a demo account.
5. Never film the avatar photo→cartoon flow with a real photo. AvatarEditorView can take a photo from the library and cartoonify it. Use only the 14 emoji avatars (🦊 🐻 🐰 🦄 🐉 🦁 🐱 🐶 🐢 🦉 🌟 🌙 ✨ 🌈) on camera. Do not show a real child's photo even mid-transformation, even blurred, even "it's my own kid."
6. If a parental gate appears ("👋 Grown-up check", an addition problem), you may show it — it is a genuine safety feature and worth showing — but never show it being bypassed or dismissed quickly. Let it read.
7. No real order details. OrdersListView / OrderDetailView / PrintOrderView contain shipping addresses and prices. Never film a real order. If print-on-demand must be shown, use a demo order with an obviously fictional address, and never show a full address on screen.
8. No sign-in credentials, no email addresses, no account IDs, no push tokens, no debug overlays.

DEVICE, FRAME AND ORIENTATION
9. iPad is the primary device. The native app is iPad-first (HeroLanding branches at ≥700×700 for the iPad layout; BookDetailView renders a genuine two-page spread only in landscape). Shoot iPad first; shoot iPhone as the secondary cut. Never let an iPhone crop stand in for the iPad experience in a post about the reading spread.
10. Use current, unbranded-by-the-designer Apple device frames at their real corner radius. Straight-on, 0° tilt. No faux-3D perspective, no floating angled mockups, no hands-holding-phone stock imagery.
11. Portrait for the bookshelf, the create wizard, Story Buddy and Account. Landscape iPad for the reader — that is where the two-page spread (illustration left, story right) exists. Rotate deliberately; do not rotate mid-take.
12. The app is dark-only — it forces .preferredColorScheme(.dark). There is no light mode to shoot. Any "light mode" post would be fabricated.
13. Record at native resolution, 60fps, then deliver at 30fps for feed. Never upscale a recording.

FRAMING FOR INSTAGRAM'S UI
14. 4:5 (1080×1350) is the default feed post; 1:1 (1080×1080) for grids and reward beats; 9:16 (1080×1920) for Stories and Reels.
15. In 9:16, keep the top 250px and bottom 420px free of anything that must be read, and leave the right-hand 180px gutter clear for the Reels action rail.
16. In 4:5, keep the bottom 220px free of critical UI — captions and the "more" truncation crowd it.
17. Never let a device frame's edge fall within 60px of the canvas edge; give it air or bleed it fully off, nothing in between.
18. Text overlaid directly on the nebula needs a rgba(0,0,0,0.35) 20px-blur backing or it will lose contrast against the #2E1252 bright stop.

WHAT MUST NOT APPEAR IN A CAPTURE
19. No empty states. Never film "No pages yet", "No books yet", the empty bookshelf, or the signed-out create screen ("Sign in to save your books..."). Populate the account first.
20. No loading states, no spinners, no "Thinking…" from Story Buddy left hanging, no AsyncImage placeholder, no broken-photo icon. If an illustration hasn't loaded, stop and re-take.
21. No error copy, no paywall unless the post is explicitly about pricing, and no partially-typed text with a caret sitting in an empty field.
22. No iOS system chrome that dates the post: hide the battery-low indicator, use a clean status bar (full signal, 100% battery, a neutral time — 9:41 is the convention), and turn off notifications entirely before recording.
23. Do not film the mascot inside the iOS app expecting the drawn character. The Assets.xcassets mascot imagesets currently declare no image files, so the app falls back to emoji (⭐ 👋 🤗 🎉 🤔 🏅). Check the actual build in hand: if you see an emoji where the mascot should be, that is what the shipping app does, and you must composite the mascot in the graphic rather than claim it appears in the UI.

MOTION AND SOUND
24. Turn Reduce Motion OFF when recording — the sparkles, confetti, nebula drift, StatPill pop and banner spring are the product's personality and are all suppressed under that setting. But do not imply motion is mandatory: the app respects Reduce Motion throughout, and that is worth a post of its own.
25. Let the beats breathe. The cheer animation runs 0.62s and stops after two bounces; the welcome-back moment holds ~4.2s; the banner springs in, holds, springs out. Do not cut on top of them, and do not loop a cheer forever — the app deliberately doesn't.
26. Page turns are horizontal swipes with a page-turn sound. Swipe at a natural child's pace, roughly one page per 1.5s, and don't scrub.
27. Capture the app's own audio where possible (page-turn, sparkle, celebrate SFX). If you add music, duck it under the app sound rather than replacing it.
28. Never speed-ramp a recording to make a feature look faster than it is — particularly anything involving AI illustration, which takes real time.

ACCURACY
29. Everything on screen must be a real, current build of ios-native/MyBookLab. No mockups presented as screenshots, no Figma comps composited into a device frame, no features "coming soon" shown as if shipped. If a screen has to be staged, stage it inside the real app with demo data.
30. Do not source colour, logos or copy from public/favicon.svg, src/theme/galaxyWonder.js, or STORE_LISTING.md — all three are stale relative to what ships.
